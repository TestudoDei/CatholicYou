# CatholicYou editorial standard

Every published entry should help a reader pray while remaining precise about what the Church teaches and what history can establish.

Before drafting or revising an entry, use this guide together with [the content model](./CONTENT_MODEL.md) and [the story-arc guide](./EDITORIAL_STORY_ARCS.md).

## Required fields

- Name, slug, feast date, calendar scope, liturgical rank, short summary
- Historical notes and devotional traditions kept distinct
- Sources with publisher, title, URL or publication data, and access date
- Editorial status and reviewer

## Review outcomes

- **Approved:** Claims are supported, terminology is sound, and devotional language is proportionate.
- **Approved with edits:** The entry is usable after listed corrections.
- **Requires human review:** Sources conflict, a quotation cannot be traced, the subject concerns private revelation, indulgences, moral theology, controversy, or apparent Church authority.

## Preferred sources

Prefer Scripture, Vatican documents, the Roman Missal and liturgical calendars, bishops’ conferences, critical editions, and reputable Catholic reference works. Avoid using unsourced devotional aggregators as factual authorities.

## Reader-facing narrative

- Give each homepage preview a complete movement from origin, to distinctive significance, to living devotion.
- When Scripture supplies little or no biography, do not lead with that absence. Pivot to the strongest supported tradition, patronage, intercession, shrine, pilgrimage, reported miracle, or devotional practice.
- Keep the evidence boundary visible through labels and proportionate phrases such as “Scripture records,” “documented history,” “tradition remembers,” and “later devotion came to identify.”
- Do not make a source say more than it says. If a tradition reports that a promised child would be born, do not claim the parents were told the child’s future identity unless the source states that explicitly.
- Distinguish the earliest recoverable form of a tradition from later devotional or artistic elaboration. Both can be meaningful, but they should not be collapsed into one historical claim.
- Prefer concrete sentences that tell the reader what happened or what can be seen. Replace abstract phrases such as “gives the tradition a human center” with the actual human action or visual detail.
- Let complete stories vary with the saint’s evidence and story arc. Do not force every life into the same lesson, conflict, or redemption pattern.
- Keep reader-facing names consistent throughout an entry. When a museum, archive, or source uses a different form of a saint’s name, preserve its exact catalog title in the source record while using CatholicYou’s chosen form in displayed copy.

## Sacred art

- Select art because it reveals something important about the saint’s reception, symbolism, devotion, or story—not simply because an image is available.
- On the homepage, identify what the viewer can see and why it matters in one clear sentence. On the complete page, explain the work more fully while distinguishing artistic interpretation from Scripture and documented history.
- Keep artwork scoring and weekly-email suitability internal. The visitor should encounter the work and its meaning, not the editorial ranking.
- Treat rights clearance as mandatory and separate from quality. Record the exact image source, museum object page, rights policy, credit line, accession number, and verification date.
